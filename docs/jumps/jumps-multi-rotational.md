# {{ $frontmatter.title }}

The last file of Chapter 8 of *The Complete Book of Figure Skating*: the doubles and triples — Salchow, toe loop, loop, flip, Lutz, and Axel, usually learned in that order — plus the double toe Walley, the quads, and the tools for learning them. The most common multi-rotational jumps are those six singles with extra rotations added, and everything in the fundamentals still applies: the instructions for each single carry over, with the actions **quicker and tighter** as the rotations increase.

::: danger Reference only — this is not a how-to page
Doubles and triples are **coach- and harness-level jumps**. This file is a reference for understanding and recognising them — the theory behind them, what each one is, and how coaches evaluate them. It deliberately gives **no step-by-step instructions to try**, and it carries **no tutorial videos**: these jumps are learned on the ice with a coach, usually with a harness, never from a page.
:::

::: info Which rotation are these pages written for?
All descriptions in the book — and in all six Jumps files — assume a skater rotating **left / counter-clockwise**. Skaters who rotate to the right reverse the starting foot and the direction throughout.
:::

## Jumping Theory <Badge type="danger" text="Advanced — reference only" /> {#jumping-theory}

The biomechanics of jumping has been explored thoroughly, and the studies agree on a few basic premises.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Body** | **Height and length are set at takeoff.** The height of a jump is proportional to the vertical force generated at takeoff, and its length to the horizontal force (speed). If horizontal force is much greater than vertical thrust — the energy of the arms, knee, ankle, and free leg — the jump is very long and not very high; if the vertical thrust is greater, the jump is high and not very long. The forces have to be integrated properly, or the energy is dissipated early or applied inefficiently. The lower and upper body must maintain the **same vertical axis**: if the free shoulder is ahead of the skating shoulder at takeoff, the jump is over-rotated at landing. |
| **Feet** | In the air position shared by all multi-rotational jumps: **half the rotation is done by the apex** of the jump; the body weight shifts from the takeoff side to the landing side; the arms pull in **across the centre of the chest with the elbows down**; the free leg is crossed in front of the skating leg, slightly above the ankle, with the free hip raised slightly higher; the head is slightly rotated toward the direction of flight. |
| **Blade** | Rotation is created on the ice, not in the air — see the angular momentum notes below. On the forward takeoff edge of an Axel there will be a **skid**: the old theory demanded no skid at all, but a **controlled skid** is now accepted as a necessary element (below). |

#### Angular momentum — the physics in four lines

- A skater exerts force against the ice to initiate rotation. Once airborne, **no significant force can change the angular momentum** — angular momentum (rotational force) equals the moment of inertia (resistance to rotation) × the angular velocity (rotational speed). The angular momentum for a triple or quad must be **generated before leaving the ice**.*
- What *can* change in flight is the speed of rotation, through body position: the farther the limbs are from the axis of rotation, the slower the rotation. **Pull in tight → rotate faster. Open up → slow down** for the landing.
- Rotational speed and flight time are two of the most important elements in jumps — the conclusion of the biomechanical analysis by Dr. Sarah Smith and Dr. Debbie King from the U.S. Olympic Committee's 1992 study, in which triple Axels were videotaped, digitised, and rebuilt as three-dimensional computer models. The work continued on the triple toe loop, triple Lutz, triple flip, and quad jumps.
- In that study, skaters reached **similar heights and spent about the same time in the air for single, double, and triple Axels** — and travelled farthest in the single, shortest in the triple. On the triple Axel, skaters reach the tight position as fast as possible by lifting the arms less and raising the free knee less than in the single or double, and angular momentum appears to be increased by lowering the arms and free leg, stepping the takeoff leg over more quickly, and increasing the skid's length and width.

*The book's own footnote: this statement has met with some controversy — the USOC research supports it, but many coaches do not agree with the theory.*

#### Skids at takeoff — Don Laws' three types

- **Increasing skid** — the most serious error, resulting in a poor jump.
- **Diminishing (decreasing) skid** — rare; may occur on a double Axel once the triple Axel has been mastered.
- **Controlled (constant) skid** — the most acceptable.

## The Progression Table <Badge type="danger" text="Advanced — reference only" /> {#the-progression-table}

One row per jump — revolutions, takeoff and landing exactly as the book states them, and the one point the book flags for that jump. Every landing below is the checked back outside landing of the fundamentals file.

| Jump | Rev. | Takeoff | Landing | Book's key point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Double Salchow | 2 | Left back inside edge (edge jump) | Right back outside | The checked position after the entry three is crucial to set up the rotation; shoulders and hips must not drop too far over the back inside takeoff edge. |
| Triple Salchow | 3 | Left back inside edge | Right back outside, deeply bent knee | Same as the double, even quicker and tighter; guard against tilting into the takeoff curve — 1½ rotations must be complete by the apex. |
| Double Toe Loop | 2 | Right back outside edge, assisted by a left toe tap | Right back outside | A toe pick placed too far to the side causes a twisted, prematurely rotated jump. |
| Triple Toe Loop | 3 | Right back outside edge, left toe tap | Right back outside | Quicker and tighter; you must be into the second rotation at the apex — triples pull in sooner and stay in longer. |
| Double Toe Walley | 2 | Right back **inside** edge, left toe placed in the ice | Right back outside | Often confused with the double toe loop — the difference is the takeoff edge (inside, not outside). The book notes the plain double Walley is rarely performed: difficult, and often unrecognised by audiences and judges. |
| Double Loop | 2 | Right back outside edge (edge jump) | Right back outside — the same edge as takeoff | Keep the left arm tightly checked in front: releasing it before takeoff brings little or no liftoff, insufficient rotation, and imbalance. All rotation must be complete before the blade hits the ice. |
| Triple Loop | 3 | Right back outside edge | Right back outside | First completed by Richard Button in 1952. Air time barely differs between single, double, and triple, so strength, speed, and quick reflexes have to supply the extra rotation; arms closer in, free leg nearly straight but still crossed at the ankle. |
| Double Flip | 2 | Left back inside edge, right toe placed in the ice | Right back outside | The entry turn (forward outside three or forward inside Mohawk) must not swing wide — it is done closer to a straight line, with matching edge lengths before and after the turn. |
| Triple Flip | 3 | Left back inside edge, right toe | Right back outside | All single and double instructions apply; the actions must be quicker and tighter. |
| Double Lutz | 2 | Left back **outside** edge, right toe placed in the ice | Right back outside | The most common error: changing from back outside to back inside just before takeoff turns it into a double flip — a **“flutz.”** Fix it by going back to the single Lutz takeoff. |
| Triple Lutz | 3 | Left back outside edge, right toe | Right back outside | Same as the double; the same back-outside-to-inside edge change is the error to guard. |
| Double Axel | 2½ | Left forward outside edge (edge jump) | Right back outside | The **transition jump into triples**. The free leg swings forward in a straight line, not around; a skid on the takeoff edge is expected but kept to a minimum — too much rotation before leaving the ice means an excessive skid; nearly half the rotation must be done by the apex. |
| Triple Axel | 3½ | Left forward outside edge | Right back outside, well-bent knee | All single and double instructions apply, with more strength, power, tightness, and quicker reflexes; the body weight tends to end up unbalanced — usually too far back — so control the entry speed and complete half the rotation by the apex. |

#### Reading the table

- **Edge or toe?** Salchow, loop, and Axel take off from an edge alone. Toe loop, toe Walley, flip, and Lutz add a toe tap or placement — which foot taps is in the takeoff column.
- **Same foot or change of foot?** Only the loop takes off *and* lands on the same foot and edge. Everything else here lands on the right back outside edge, whatever it took off from.
- **As rotations increase, so must strength, power, tightness of position, and quick reflexes** — that sentence, repeated for the triple Salchow, triple Axel, and the quads, is the book's whole method for adding a rotation. Upper body strength matters in all triple jumps: the book prescribes supervised off-ice exercises and free weights, concentrating on the upper body, hamstrings, and quads.

## Master the Move — Brian Boitano's Picture-Perfect Jumps <Badge type="danger" text="Advanced — reference only" /> {#master-the-move-brian-boitano}

::: info Master the Move
Brian Boitano's jumping technique is **the standard by which to judge and critique jumps** — his jumps are nearly textbook perfect. His air position is the model the theory section describes: straight from head to toes, with square hips and shoulders, and tight arms and legs.
:::

![Book photo: Brian Boitano high in the air in a tight, crossed air position, arms pulled in across his chest](/jumps-multi-assets/boitano-jumps.png)
*From the book — 'Master the Move': Brian Boitano's picture-perfect jumps (photo courtesy of Leah Adams)*

- Boitano also created variations of standard jumps. The **“Tano”** is his trademark: a triple Lutz executed with **one arm raised above the head** while the other rests on the waist — a dramatic position on a jump that is difficult enough without it, and the trademark of the 1988 Olympic champion.
- With so much emphasis on jumping — and with quads (and, as the book puts it, even quints) on the horizon — distinguishing yourself takes the technique **plus** hard work, discipline, and innovation.

## Quads <Badge type="danger" text="Advanced — reference only" /> {#quads}

The book gives the quadruple jumps **no separate descriptions**: the basic instructions for the corresponding double and triple apply to each quad. As rotation increases, the same elements are essential:

- Tighter, more streamlined positions
- Quicker reflexes
- Increased strength
- Greater power and speed

**Off-ice conditioning and strength training are mandatory.** The quads, hamstrings, glutes, abdominals, and upper body muscles must all be trained for strength and quickness.

## Tools for Learning Jumps <Badge type="danger" text="Advanced — reference only" /> {#tools-for-learning-jumps}

The chapter's closing toolbox — useful for beginners getting comfortable with first jumps, and for advanced skaters refining multi-rotational ones.

| Tool | What the book says |
|---|---|
| **Harness** | An **invaluable** aid for introducing Axels and double and triple jumps: properly used, it develops timing, muscle memory in performing and landing a jump, and confidence. It is still only a tool. If your rink has no installed harness, a hand-held harness is useful — and a coach who has never used one should ask another coach for assistance before putting a student on it. |
| **Impact pads** | Protect against injury when falling repeatedly on jumps. Too cumbersome to wear all the time, but often an aid while learning a jump. |
| **Video camera** | Videotaping is a wonderful learning and teaching aid: skate the jump, view it immediately, and review it with a coach, who points out strengths and weaknesses. Some rinks provide a rink-side camera during freestyle sessions. The Olympic Training Center's elite camp goes further — biomechanical analysis computing free leg position, takeoff angle, vertical and horizontal velocity, rotational velocity, time to attain the rotating position, and jump height. Ordinary coaches can still review video against the same checkpoints. |
| **Off-ice training** | Off-ice jumping is extremely helpful for teaching and perfecting jumps in a non-threatening environment; off-ice conditioning is a necessity for reaching maximum jumping potential, and a great protector against injury. All off-ice training should be supervised by a trained professional. |

#### The five evaluation criteria

A skater, assisted by a coach, can evaluate each jump on:

1. Preparation or setup
2. Takeoff
3. Air position
4. Landing
5. Speed

#### Six common jumping errors — Don Laws

Don Laws, coach of Olympic champion Scott Hamilton, suggests looking for:

1. Excessive lean in the airborne position
2. Imbalanced ratio of jump height to jump distance
3. Pounding of toe in toe jump takeoff
4. Excessive curve of edge at takeoff
5. Pelvic area off the line of balance
6. Lack of rhythmic process

And six **qualities** to look for instead: **speed, height, distance, posture, stability, consistency.**

## Summary <Badge type="info" text="Chapter close" /> {#summary}

The chapter's own summary, in brief:

- Whatever techniques you add as jumps get harder, remember the basics: **speed, timing, and rhythm are necessities**, and consistent approaches and setups are key factors.
- Successful jumps rely on strong, correct execution at **three critical junctures: takeoff, air position, and landing**.
- Keep jumps in perspective. They may be the most exciting element in a program, but not all skaters are gifted jumpers, and judges constantly encourage skaters **not to overload a program with elements that cannot be executed effectively** — a high, strong single jump is better than a small double jump that is cheated and barely leaves the ground.
