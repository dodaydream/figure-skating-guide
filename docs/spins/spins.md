# {{ $frontmatter.title }}

A well-balanced program rests on three elements — jumps, spins, and footwork — and spins are finally getting the credit jumps always got. From Chapter 7 of *The Complete Book of Figure Skating*: the fundamentals every spin shares, then the families — upright, sit, camel, layback, flying, and the illusion / star / butterfly group.

::: tip The golden rule
Every spin has the same goal: **fast, controlled, centred, and in a good position** — rotating over one spot, never travelling across the ice. Speed comes later; a centred spin you can hold beats a fast spin that wanders.
:::

There are four basic spins — the **upright**, the **sit**, the **camel**, and the **layback** — and the innovative positions (the Biellmann is the famous one) all build on them. A spin is a balance of two forces: **centripetal** force, which you feel as you draw your arms and legs in tightly toward the centre of your body, and **centrifugal** force, which pushes them away. Beat the outward pull by drawing in, and the spin accelerates.

::: info Flat spins and toe spins
Spins are also classified by the part of the blade you spin on. **Flat spins** turn just behind the toe pick, on the front part of the blade — camel and sit spins and their variations. **Toe spins** turn farther forward, catching the first toe pick — one-footed, scratch, and layback spins and their variations.
:::

::: info Which direction?
Decide once whether you spin clockwise or counter-clockwise — and jump and spin in the **same** direction. Everything in these pages follows the book, which describes spins rotating to the **left (counter-clockwise)**; reverse the descriptions if you rotate to the right.
:::

How to read the diagrams: each spin is shown in three keyframes — **1 · Entry, 2 · The spin, 3 · Exit** — looking down at your own feet. The entry print is a curve that spirals into the centre; the spin stage sits centred over the tight little circles it prints on the ice; the exit glides out on a back outside edge. Red dot = the edge doing the work; pale blue = the print left on the ice; a dashed boot is the free foot, lifted off the ice. Exceptions are noted where they occur: in the two-footed and cross foot spins both boots are on the ice by design.

## Spin Entry {#spin-entry}

Almost every spin in this chapter begins the same way, from **back crossovers** — learn this entry once and it serves them all.

![Book photos: (a) a skater holding the right back inside edge, free leg extended behind; (b) stepping into the circle on a bent knee](/spins-assets/fundamentals/fig71-spin-entry.png)
*From the book — Figure 7.1: spin entry — (a) the right back inside edge is held, then (b) the left foot steps into the circle*

![Book diagram: the correct entry steps into the circle off the inside edge; the incorrect one steps out of the circle](/spins-assets/fundamentals/fig72-step-into-circle.png)
*From the book — Figure 7.2: step into the circle, not out of it*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Body** | Shoulders level, skating knee well bent. The knee stays bent until **after** the entry three has hooked the spin, then straightens once one full turn is completed, balance kept forward on the blade. |
| **Feet** | Hold the **right back inside edge** of the last crossover, free leg extended behind the body in line with the skating foot — then the left leg **steps into the circle** formed by that edge's curve. |
| **Blade** | A small, tight **three turn** occurs at the entry of every spin: it **"hooks"** the spin, catching the **first tooth of the toe pick** on a back inside edge. |

#### Tips

- **Step into the circle off the inside edge — never out of the circle** (Figure 7.2). Stepping out is the beginner's classic error and the spin never centres.
- An alternate entry exists — from a right forward inside three, stepping onto a left forward outside edge — but it is less common and harder for a beginning skater.

## Centring {#centring}

The most important part of a spin. A centred spin means the body is in perfect balance over the blade, shoulders and hips **square**, back erect — and the spin remains in **one spot**.

![Book diagram: a centred spin prints small circles beside the entry three; a travelling spin prints looping circles spread across the ice](/spins-assets/fundamentals/fig73-centering-circles.png)
*From the book — Figure 7.3: the spin must be skated in circles, not loops*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Body** | Shoulders level and **down**; hips level and **square**; weight kept over the skating side, leaning into the entry-edge circle. |
| **Feet** | The free leg stays controlled, stretched, and held firmly **behind** the body until the point of the three — and the arms and free leg reach their final position only **after** the spin is hooked. |
| **Blade** | The entry edge must be a **strong, deep curve whose diameter progressively diminishes** as it approaches the three. Its print: small concentric circles placed closely beside the entry three. A travelling spin prints tiny **loops** spread across the ice — the more it travels, the wider the loops spread. |

#### Tips — why spins travel

- A **shallow entry edge**, or one that doesn't progressively diminish in diameter.
- Letting the **free leg swing around before the point of the turn** — the spin starts before the entry edge has diminished enough.
- Once rotation starts, don't relax the arms and free leg: centrifugal force will pull them out and drag the centre with them. The skating foot should make smooth, even, little circles.

## Arm Positions {#arm-positions}

There are two schools of thought on the arms at the entry. Knowing both lets you rescue a spin by switching methods.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Method 1 — set on the entry** *(most common)* | Bring the **left arm across in front** of the body with the **right arm behind**, counter-rotated. The lead (skating-side) arm stays forward over the print as you step in; as the three is hooked, the arm rotates into its final position. The arm is set early and **generates momentum** as you step in. Keep the arms on an even plane — dropped shoulders unbalance the start, and an arm allowed to swing across the body can make the spin travel. |
| **Method 2 — rotate before the step** | While still on the back inside edge, rotate the arms first: the **right arm leads across in front** and over the print, pulling the **left arm strongly behind**, so you step in with the shoulders already rotated and the hips square to the print. Balance can't be offset by a swinging arm — but generating the initial momentum is harder. |

## Speed of Rotation {#speed-of-rotation}

Beyond centring and control, **velocity** is the most important element of a good spin — and the more streamlined the body, the greater the opportunity for speed. A scratch spin will obviously out-spin a camel.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Body** | For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: maintain a **lifting and stretching** opposed by a **pulling and tightening**. The primary motions that increase speed are **in and down**. |
| **Feet** | Hips stay **over the spinning foot** at all times. |
| **Blade** | Once the position is struck, hold the balance over the blade: any rocking back and forth on the blade **immediately diminishes speed**. The only recovery is to open the position up and pull in again. |

#### The three essentials of angular velocity

1. The arms must pull **toward the rotational axis**.
2. The hips must be kept **over the spinning foot**.
3. The head must be kept **erect** (in an upright spin).

#### Tips — dizziness

- Don't focus on objects as they spin past. Accomplished skaters — the technique NASA confirmed by studying them — focus on the **space between themselves and the object**. With practice and skill, dizziness diminishes significantly.
- The more speed you generate at the **beginning** of the spin, the longer it will be held — provided balance is maintained. Like a top, a skater who loses balance on a fast spin flies out of control.

## Exit {#exit}

Every spin in this chapter finishes the same way, whatever family it belongs to.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Body** | Body erect, head up, arms out to the side — the same final position as a jump landing, and **held** to demonstrate balance and control. |
| **Feet** | The free foot is extended in the finish position; the skating knee is bent. |
| **Blade** | Push off onto a **right back outside edge**, exactly as for a right back outside push-off. |
